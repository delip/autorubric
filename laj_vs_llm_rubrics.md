Task: Review the latest "LLM as a judge" literature and latest literature on "Evaluating LLM outputs using Rubrics", and contrast the two.

Objective
We review two related paradigms for automated evaluation of LLM outputs: (1) LLM-as-a-Judge (LAJ), where an LLM provides holistic judgments, and (2) rubric-based LLM evaluation, where criteria are explicit and operationalized (locked rubrics, checklists, evidence anchoring). We synthesize reliability, bias, calibration, robustness, and meta-evaluation results, contrast when each approach works or fails, and conclude with best practices.

Core findings, with quantitative evidence
- Reliability and bias in LAJ. Meta-evaluations report substantial instability and bias. Self-reliability studies show the same LAJ can disagree with itself across identical runs; only ~18% of surveyed papers report proper agreement analyses, underscoring the need to measure intra-judge agreement and aggregate multiple samples or panels (e.g., SummEval, MT-Bench, JudgeBench) ([1.1]). Design audits of judge benchmarks reveal large unexplained variance: on Arena-Hard-Auto, roughly 55% of judgment variance is not explained by rubric criteria on average, with some models exceeding 90%; factor correlations >0.93 indicate poor discriminant validity among rubric dimensions, and ELO/Bradley-Terry aggregations can mask non-transitive preferences and uncertainty ([2.1]). Surveys note common biases (position, empty-reference) and recommend temperature=0 and multi-round aggregation to reduce randomness; ensemble voting can worsen certain biases depending on model mix ([3.1]).

- Calibration and robustness methods for LAJ. Methods using distributional outputs rather than single decoded judgments, selective evaluation with confidence guarantees, and checklist-style decompositions are gaining traction. Distributional inference and multi-round summarization improve stability; selective evaluation frameworks calibrate confidence and "trust or escalate" to stronger judges while guaranteeing specified levels of human agreement; lightweight judges benefit from decomposing tasks into instance-specific checklists and reweighting to supervised labels, reaching correlations up to 0.965 against human preferences on MT-Bench and WildBench while reducing cost by >50x ([3.1], [4.1]).

- Rubric-based evaluation: explicit criteria, locking, and evidence anchoring. RULERS compiles natural-language rubrics into locked, executable specifications with checklist items and evidence rules, enforces extractive, evidence-anchored reasoning, and applies post-hoc score distribution alignment. Reported QWK against human scoring: 0.7276 (ASAP2.0 essays), 0.3367 (SummHF summarization), 0.5206 (DREsS); ablations show calibration is crucial (ASAP2.0 QWK drops to 0.2643 without WGR), and rubric locking and evidence verification also contribute materially. RULERS is more robust to rubric perturbations (reversed/paraphrased) than baselines, addressing rubric instability and unverifiable rationales observed in generic LAJ ([5.1], [5.2]). Checklist-driven pipelines for lightweight judges (RocketEval) achieve high human correlation (0.965 MT-Bench/WildBench) with explicit instance-specific criteria and item reweighting ([4.1]).

- Domain/meta-evaluation insights. A web-development meta-evaluation built around query-grounded rubrics and paired human preferences achieved 89.7% human inter-annotator agreement, yet LAJ models still lagged human preferences by >15% and struggled with functional equivalence and interactive feasibility verification, showing that even rubric-grounded ground truths can expose persistent gaps in automated judges ([6.1]).

Contrast: LAJ versus rubric-based evaluation
- Methodological stance. LAJ emphasizes holistic, task-general judgments in natural language; it is scalable but susceptible to prompt/position/reference biases and to instability across runs. Rubric-based evaluation formalizes criteria into locked specifications (checklists, evidence rules) that constrain judgment and improve reproducibility and auditability ([2.1], [5.1], [5.2]).

- Reliability and reproducibility. LAJ exhibits notable self-inconsistency and large unexplained variance vis-a-vis intended rubrics; naive aggregation (e.g., ELO) can obscure uncertainty. Rubric-based pipelines report stronger stability under rubric perturbations and clearer links between evidence and scores; however, rubric quality and domain coverage remain limiting factors, and calibration to human score distributions is often essential ([1.1], [2.1], [5.1]).

- Bias and robustness. LAJ commonly shows position and empty-reference biases and can be sensitive to explanation prompts and ensemble composition; mitigation includes randomization, order swapping, multi-round aggregation, and selective evaluation with confidence guarantees. Rubric-based approaches explicitly encode criteria and require extractive evidence, reducing susceptibility to spurious features and unverifiable rationales; if rubrics are underspecified or misaligned with human standards, locking can preserve those flaws ([3.1], [2.1], [5.1], [5.2]).

- Calibration. LAJ benefits from distributional inference and confidence-calibrated selective evaluation; rubric-based evaluation often employs post-hoc distribution alignment (e.g., Wasserstein-based generative regression) and reweighting of checklist items to match human labels, which is empirically pivotal for human alignment ([3.1], [4.1], [5.1]).

- Domain transfer, interactive and complex tasks. LAJ is attractive for new domains due to low upfront rubric engineering, but meta-evaluations show gaps in detecting functional equivalence and handling interactive evaluations. Rubric-guided pipelines can yield high human agreement in building ground truth and more diagnostic analysis, yet still reveal significant model-human gaps in complex, interactive tasks ([6.1]).

- Transparency and auditability. Rubric-based methods (locked specs, evidence anchoring) strengthen transparency and enable schematic-adherence diagnostics; LAJ requires additional diagnostics to verify rubric adherence and to surface uncertainty, as variance analyses show a large portion of decisions not attributable to stated criteria ([2.1], [5.1]).

- Security and reproducibility. Design recommendations emphasize procedural defenses (randomization, tie-handling, manual review of low-confidence cases) and transparency of prompts and explanations. Rubric locking and structured checklists also improve reproducibility and reduce prompt-sensitivity ([7.1], [5.1], [4.1]).

> Best-practice checklist
> - Set judge configuration for stability: use temperature=0 for deterministic baseline and consider multi-round aggregation (e.g., 5 rounds) to reduce randomness ([3.1]).
> - Measure and report self-reliability (intra-judge agreement); mitigate self-inconsistency with sampling-based aggregation (e.g., temp=1, 20 samples) or panel/ensemble judgments rather than a single run ([1.1]).
> - Check and mitigate ordering/reference biases by swapping/randomizing option positions and treating inconsistent outcomes as ties; monitor for empty-reference effects ([3.1], [7.1]).
> - Validate judge adherence to intended rubrics using diagnostic metrics (schematic adherence, psychometric validity); avoid blind ELO/BT aggregation that can mask uncertainty or non-transitive preferences ([2.1]).
> - For rubric-driven evaluation, compile and lock rubrics into explicit, versioned checklists and require extractive, evidence-anchored reasoning from judges to improve reproducibility (RULERS approach) ([5.2], [5.1]).
> - Calibrate score distributions post-hoc (distributional alignment / WGR-style calibration) to map judge outputs to human scoring scales ([5.1]).
> - When using lightweight judges, decompose evaluation into instance-specific checklist items, auto-grade each item, then reweight items to supervised labels to recover high human correlation at lower cost (RocketEval) ([4.1]).
> - Maintain human-in-the-loop safeguards: spot-check low-confidence or edge-case judgments, iteratively refine rubrics (hierarchical dimensions and few-shot anchors), and keep human oversight for high-stakes or underspecified criteria ([7.1], [4.1]).

Recommendations (evidence-backed)
- For LAJ pipelines: stabilize and validate. Configure judges with temperature=0 for a deterministic baseline and use multi-round aggregation to reduce randomness; measure self-reliability and aggregate via sampling or panel-based approaches; randomize/swap positions to detect and mitigate position bias; avoid blind ELO/BT aggregation and report schematic adherence and psychometric validity; consider selective evaluation with confidence guarantees and escalate difficult cases ([3.1], [1.1], [2.1]).
- For rubric-based pipelines: make criteria executable and calibrate. Lock rubrics as versioned checklists with explicit evidence rules; require extractive, evidence-anchored reasoning; apply post-hoc distribution alignment (e.g., WGR) and item reweighting against human labels; use instance-specific checklists to empower lightweight judges when cost or privacy are constraints; maintain human oversight where rubrics are underspecified ([5.1], [5.2], [4.1]).
- For domains with interaction or equivalence (e.g., web development): build query-grounded rubric datasets with high-quality paired preferences and use them for meta-evaluation; expect remaining >15% model-human gaps and probe functional-equivalence detection and feasibility verification explicitly ([6.1]).

Limitations and open directions
Rubric-based stability depends on rubric completeness; locking an imperfect rubric preserves its blind spots. LAJ remains attractive for rapid, cross-domain evaluation but needs calibrated, selective, and distributional inference plus strong diagnostics to ensure reliability. Future work should integrate schematic-adherence diagnostics into LAJ pipelines, extend evidence-anchored scoring beyond text to interactive and multimodal settings, and improve calibration methods that generalize across domains and tasks ([2.1], [5.1], [6.1]).
